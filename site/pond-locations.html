Pond bubbling and standing-water observations, 2018–2026

This page brings together five documented pond or standing-water locations recorded during surveys in July 2018 and July 2026. Each mapped point links to the available high-resolution imagery and, where available, video showing the water surface in motion.

Bubbles in ponds can arise through several processes. A common mechanism is ebullition, in which gas generated or accumulated within waterlogged sediment escapes through the sediment and rises through the water column. Methane produced by microbial decomposition under oxygen-poor conditions is frequently involved, although bubbles may contain mixtures of gases and their composition cannot be determined visually. Bubble release can vary greatly between ponds, within a single pond, and over time, and may become concentrated at persistent or repeatedly reopened outlets in the sediment.

Other possible explanations for localised bubbling include gas-bearing groundwater discharge, disturbance of sediment, decomposition of organic material, animal activity, or the release of trapped air. Video and still imagery can establish the visible occurrence, position and surface behaviour of bubbling, but cannot by themselves establish gas composition, depth of origin, hydraulic pathway or relationship to nearby operations.

What the archive currently establishes

On 27 July 2018, visible localised surface disturbance consistent with bubbling was recorded at three separate ponds during the same aerial survey. The original high-resolution files retain their date, time and GPS metadata. One of the locations was also recorded on video and was subsequently included in correspondence concerning pond-water issues.

In July 2026, bubbling was recorded at two further locations:

  • an established pond near the Preston New Road pad, identified in the combined scarp, depressions and pond-associated change report; and
  • persistent standing water within a field near Anna’s Road, at a location that is not an established pond and which remained wet during drought conditions.

The teardrop pond was not observed bubbling in the available 2018 imagery. Its documented bubbling in 2026 therefore represents a change in the recorded condition rather than a continuation demonstrated by the presently reviewed archive.

The archive is not yet sufficiently reviewed to determine whether bubbling occurred at additional dates or locations but went unnoticed at the time. Coverage beyond the original primary study zone is sparse, and a more detailed image and video review is required before conclusions can be drawn about frequency, persistence or wider distribution.

Evidential limits

The material establishes:

  • the visible occurrence of bubbling or localised ripple generation;
  • the recorded date and mapped location of each observation;
  • three documented bubbling ponds on one survey date in 2018;
  • two documented bubbling or gas-release locations in 2026; and
  • a change in the recorded behaviour of the teardrop pond between the reviewed 2018 and 2026 material.

It does not establish:

  • the identity or concentration of the gas;
  • whether the same process operated at every location;
  • whether the ponds are hydraulically connected;
  • the depth or source of the gas; or
  • any causal relationship with hydrocarbon operations.

The purpose of this page is to preserve the observations in a form suitable for regulatory and academic review, and to identify where direct sampling, repeat observation and hydrogeological interpretation would be needed.
